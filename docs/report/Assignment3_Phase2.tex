\begin{abstract}
We implement and audit a leakage free preprocessing and evaluation pipeline that predicts a positive malaria rapid diagnostic test (RDT) from household, demographic and climate covariates in the 2023 Rwanda Malaria Indicator Survey (MIS). Every planned Phase~1 decision is compared with what the data forced in Phase~2 and backed by a number and a figure; several decisions changed, including the missing code convention, the treatment of structural missingness, the winsorizing rule for zero spread features and a mutual information tie-breaker in the collinearity screen. The matrix grows from $18{,}672\times53$ to $13{,}100\times96$ (train) with no missing cells. Positives are rare (0.96\%) and concentrated in a few clusters, so a single hold-out is unreliable; the headline result is a repeated $5\times5$ cluster grouped out-of-fold evaluation over all 179 positives. A regularized logistic regression on household features plus cluster mean context reaches PR-AUC $0.080\pm0.032$ (8.3 times the $0.0096$ baseline) and ROC-AUC $0.748\pm0.013$, whereas boosted trees reach $0.025$ to $0.031$. Pooling MIS~2017 with a survey round indicator and covariate shift weights does not help because the two rounds are perfectly separable, a compact 33 column model raises events per variable from 1.8 to 4.3 but loses ranking skill, and interview month climate features from CHIRPS, ERA5-Land and MODIS do not improve the result. Intervals are wide, and the data still needed are reported. The original project proposal is reproduced in Appendix~A.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Malaria remains a public health concern in Rwanda even after large reductions in transmission \cite{habyarimana2020}. The task is binary: for a person who was tested, predict RDT positivity (\texttt{hml35}) so that bed nets and spraying can be targeted. Phase~1 fixed a preprocessing plan before any data were seen. Phase~2 executes it in Python inside \texttt{scikit-learn} \texttt{Pipeline} and \texttt{ColumnTransformer} objects \cite{pedregosa2011} and records where reality disagreed. All statistics below are computed on training data only unless stated. Source code, figures and notebook are in the repository behind the GitHub Link under the title.

\section{Data and Pipeline Output Verification}
\textbf{Data.} The primary dataset is the Person Recode of MIS~2023 with cluster level geospatial covariates (5~km rasters, 2020 values) joined by cluster \cite{dhsguide}. The file has 19{,}038 rows; 366 have no valid test and are removed (the target is never imputed), leaving $N=18{,}672$ with $P=53$ features (26 continuous, 22 nominal, 4 discrete, 1 ordinal). Overall missingness is 1.77\% of cells; 9{,}933 rows are complete and 8{,}739 have at least one missing value.

\textbf{Split.} The hold-out is a 70/30 cluster grouped split stratified by cluster burden tier (clusters with 0, 1--2 and 3 or more positives), so every survey cluster is on one side only (overlap $=0$): train $13{,}100$ rows in 119 clusters with 138 positives (1.053\%, imbalance 93.9:1) and test $5{,}572$ rows in 51 clusters with 41 positives (0.736\%). The seed (42) was fixed in advance and never searched, which removes the split selection optimism of the earlier 80/20 design that picked seed 51 because seed 42 gave only 20 test positives. The three largest clusters (39, 14 and 11 positives) all fall in the training part, which is why the test part holds 41 rather than the targeted 50 to 55 positives and a lower prevalence. Because any single hold-out depends on a few clusters, headline results use repeated $5\times5$ cluster grouped cross validation (Sec.~\ref{sec:res}).

\textbf{Dimension verification.} Table~\ref{tab:dims} reports the matrix before and after the pipeline. No rows are deleted by outlier handling, and no information is discarded: all 53 raw features are retained and expanded to 96 model columns (ordinal and one hot encoding of 22 nominal columns, plus missing indicator flags). The correlation and VIF screen is applied \emph{only} on the linear model branch and removes 10 climate or terrain features, giving 86 columns there. PCA is used for diagnostics only; raw interpretable features are kept for classification.

\section{Empirical Justification}
\subsection{Missingness (Fig.~\ref{fig:miss}, \ref{fig:target})}
Only \texttt{hml4} and \texttt{hml10} exceed the 1\% threshold (44.05\% each); their missingness is exactly the households with no bed net. Little's test on the numeric block \cite{little1988} rejects MCAR ($\chi^2=5997.2$, df $=29$, $p<0.001$, two patterns), and Cram\'er's $V$ of the missing indicator with other variables is 1.00 (\texttt{hml12}), 0.70 (\texttt{hv227}), 0.33 (province), 0.16 (wealth), 0.03 (residence) and 0.04 (sex). The value is absent because of a skip rule, so imputing it by the mean or median would invent bed nets. Figure~\ref{fig:target} shows the 93.9:1 imbalance in training, which fixes the metric choice (PR-AUC, not accuracy).

\subsection{Outliers (Fig.~\ref{fig:out})}
\texttt{Nightlights\_Composite} has skew 3.48 and kurtosis 12.04 (Q1 $=0$, Q3 $=0.134$, fences $[-0.200,\,0.333]$) and 2{,}628 flagged values (20.06\%). Age is mildly skewed (0.87; fences $[-34.5,\,81.5]$; 88 values, 0.67\%) and rooms per household has 617 flagged values (4.71\%). Capping keeps $N$ intact, which matters with 138 training positives. The \texttt{Frost\_Days\_2020} case is a genuine deviation: with Q1 $=$ Q3 $=0$ the fence is $[0,0]$ and the plain rule would remove all non-zero information, so the winsorizer uses percentile bounds when IQR is zero.

\subsection{Association and collinearity (Figs.~\ref{fig:corr}--\ref{fig:imp})}
Nineteen pairs reach $|r|\ge0.85$, the strongest being mean temperature with PET ($r=0.993$), maximum temperature ($0.988$) and minimum temperature ($0.981$), and precipitation with aridity ($0.981$). Before screening, 14 of 30 numeric features have VIF above 10 (maximum finite VIF about $4.3\times10^{12}$ for maximum temperature, $2.3\times10^{5}$ for mean temperature). Mutual information (MI) with the target is small and tightly bunched (0.0148 to 0.0170 nats). A single kNN MI estimate has a seed to seed spread of about 0.0006, as large as the gaps between features, so MI is averaged over 10 seeds. The previous screen kept the feature with the larger $|r|$ to the target, and so dropped land surface temperature although it had the highest single seed MI (0.0162). The screen now keeps the feature with the higher averaged MI in each collinear pair, and when VIF exceeds 10 it drops the least informative feature among those over the threshold. It removes 10 features (nine by the $|r|$ rule with MI tie-break, \texttt{Wet\_Days\_2020} at VIF $=17.9$), the maximum VIF falls to 8.47, and the two highest MI features (mean temperature, 0.0170, and precipitation, 0.0169) are retained. Land surface temperature (0.0166) still yields to its $|r|=0.98$ twin, daytime land surface temperature (0.0167); the two differ by less than the estimator noise, so either is a valid representative. Tree models are not screened. Multiple tests are corrected with Benjamini--Hochberg \cite{benjamini1995}; rankings are used for screening, not inference, because clustered data make naive $p$ values optimistic.

\subsection{Transformations and PCA (Figs.~\ref{fig:tf}, \ref{fig:pca})}
For \texttt{Livestock\_Cattle}, log1p reduces skew from 3.63 to 0.13 (mean 49.4 to 3.59, sd 51.9 to 0.79); min-max gives mean 0.50, sd 0.17; standardization gives mean 0, sd 1. PCA on the 30 scaled numeric features has leading eigenvalues $11.52, 4.10, 2.45, 1.77, 1.63$ (variance $38.4\%, 13.7\%, 8.2\%, 5.9\%, 5.4\%$); 12 components reach 90\% and 15 reach 95\%. This shows strong redundancy among climate variables, but raw features are retained for interpretability.

\section{Leakage Free Architecture}
All steps are inside \texttt{Pipeline} and \texttt{ColumnTransformer} objects (sentinel recoder, winsorizers, imputers with indicators, log1p, scaler, rare level grouping, one hot and ordinal encoders, optional collinearity screen). Training skewness decides each column's branch, and every threshold, median, mean, standard deviation and capping bound is learned from \texttt{X\_train} only and applied unchanged to test data. Cross validation uses repeated cluster grouped, label stratified folds. Calibrators and operating thresholds are fit on out-of-fold predictions. The second malaria test, test-process variables, modelled malaria columns, anemia and treatment variables, identifiers are excluded as leakage, and the survey weight is used only in evaluation, never as a feature.

\section{Model Results, Diagnosis and Improvement}
\label{sec:res}
\textbf{Hold-out results.} Candidate models are regularized logistic regression, random forest, LightGBM \cite{ke2017} and XGBoost \cite{chen2016}, all with class weights; probabilities are calibrated with Platt scaling \cite{platt1999}. Table~\ref{tab:res} gives the 30\% cluster grouped hold-out. Random forest has the best hold-out PR-AUC (0.0326; weighted 0.0320) and LightGBM the best ROC-AUC (0.760), but with 41 positives the 95\% intervals (for example $[0.008, 0.079]$ for random forest) overlap every model and the ordering differs from the repeated cross validation below, so we do not rank models on this split.

\textbf{Diagnosis.} The weak hold-out scores are not a coding error. Grouped splits remove cluster memorization, and the cause is the data. Only 58 of 170 clusters contain any positive; one cluster holds 39 of 179 positives (21.8\%) and the top three hold 35.8\%. Prevalence by province code ranges from 0.35\% to 2.08\%, by age from 0.68\% to 1.22\%. A 41 positive hold-out therefore reflects a handful of clusters; the three largest all fall in training, so test prevalence (0.74\%) is below training prevalence (1.05\%). Climate covariates are constant within a cluster, so a flexible model can memorize the 170 distinct climate profiles, which does not transfer to unseen clusters. A model predicting which clusters contain a positive reaches ROC-AUC only $0.52\pm0.03$ (logistic) and $0.58\pm0.03$ (random forest).

\textbf{Headline evaluation: repeated cross validation.} To use all 179 positives and to avoid choosing a favourable seed, every person is predicted once per repeat by a model that never saw their cluster, using 5 repeats of 5-fold \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} (seeds 100 to 104, fixed in advance) over 18{,}672 rows (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}); metrics are averaged over repeats and the standard deviation across repeats is reported. Dropping the climate covariates and keeping 30 household features raises logistic PR-AUC from 0.028 to 0.070; adding cluster mean age, wealth score, household size, rooms and nets (label free context) raises it to \textbf{0.080 $\pm$ 0.032} (ROC-AUC $0.748\pm0.013$; the top 20\% highest scores capture 57\% of positives, a lift of 2.9). The large spread across repeats (individual partitions give 0.05 to 0.13) shows how strongly PR-AUC depends on which clusters share a fold; ROC-AUC is much more stable. Boosted trees do not benefit from the extra features (0.025 to 0.031) and are less stable across partitions of the same data than logistic regression is across features, consistent with cluster memorization.

\textbf{Training and test sizes, and events per variable.} A 20\% hold-out contained only 36 positives from few clusters (95\% PR-AUC interval $[0.005,0.064]$) and its seed had been chosen by label balance, so we replaced it by a burden tier stratified 70/30 split with a fixed seed (41 test positives) and, for headline claims, by the repeated cross validation above. The events per variable (EPV) ratio is the second bottleneck: with about 143 training positives, the full feature sets give EPV 1.5 to 1.9, far below the usual benchmark of 10 \cite{peduzzi1996}. A compact set that drops the seven nominal features with more than three levels (water source, wall, toilet, province, cooking fuel, floor, net type) has 33 columns (EPV 4.3), and 28 columns without cluster means (EPV 5.1). Ranking skill falls (logistic ROC-AUC 0.70 to 0.71 against 0.75), so the excluded housing and geography columns carry signal that cluster means do not replace. We keep the regularized household plus cluster mean model because it ranks best, and state plainly that its EPV is low; L2 regularization limits but does not remove the overfitting risk. The principled fix is more events, for example the RBC monthly district case series.

\textbf{Survey round pooling.} Adding MIS~2017 (1{,}765 positives) lowers logistic PR-AUC from 0.070 to 0.023 (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}, D). A domain classifier trained on household covariates separates the two rounds with cross validated AUC 1.00, mainly through the altitude indicator (\texttt{hv040} is missing for every 2017 row because MIS~2017 has no GPS) and cooking fuel codes (\texttt{hv226}, coded differently across rounds). Adding an explicit survey round indicator (E) and importance weights from the domain classifier (F; weights $p(2023|x)/p(2017|x)$ fitted on training folds only, normalized and clipped to $[0.1,10]$) does not recover performance (logistic 0.023 and 0.022), because weighting cannot transport rows between covariate distributions that do not overlap or are coded differently. Training on 2017 alone and testing on all of 2023 gives PR-AUC 0.015 to 0.021. For DHS 2019--20 we will harmonize variable codes first, then use a round indicator with covariate shift weights.

\textbf{Stage 2: tuning, ensembling and robustness.} We then tested whether tuning could beat the fixed setting. Hyperparameters were chosen by an inner cluster grouped 3-fold search on each outer training fold, with the whole procedure repeated for two split seeds (Table~\ref{tab:stage2}). L2 and elastic net logistic regression, shallow gradient boosting, a rank average ensemble, and an extended set of label free cluster context features (means of all numeric household variables, wealth spread, cluster size) all scored below the fixed logistic regression on ROC-AUC, and none exceeded it on PR-AUC. The tuned scores are lower partly because the selected penalty differed across folds (from $C=0.001$ to $0.3$), which makes pooled out-of-fold scores less comparable; this is a likely explanation and was not isolated. A sweep of the penalty strength with the same folds shows ROC-AUC flat between 0.73 and 0.75 for $C$ from 0.003 to 1, while PR-AUC moves between 0.036 and 0.082 on one seed and peaks at different values on the other, so differences between settings are within split noise. Averaged over the two seeds, the fixed model ($C=0.1$) reaches PR-AUC 0.059 and ROC-AUC 0.745, inside the range of the repeated cross validation estimate of $0.080\pm0.032$ (individual partitions vary from 0.05 to 0.13, so the repeated mean is the number to quote). We therefore keep the pre-specified model and report that tuning gave no reliable gain.

\section{Limitations and Supervisor Feedback}
\label{sec:lim}
We address the supervisor comments one by one. \textbf{Reanalysis climate and temporal mismatch.} The ERA5-Land files contained dewpoint only (no 2~m temperature or total precipitation), MODIS EVI and NDVI have 10.6\% missing values after quality filtering, and the interviews cover only three months (October to December 2023), so climate varies mainly between clusters and flexible models can memorize cluster specific noise. This is the likely reason climate features lowered PR-AUC. We keep the DHS 5~km covariates (2020 values) in the main models, and list the full ERA5-Land download (temperature and precipitation) and DHS 2019--20 as the priority acquisitions before Phase~3 (Table~\ref{tab:data}); we have not yet re-downloaded them. \textbf{Collinearity screen.} The screen now ranks by mutual information instead of $|r|$ to the target in both steps (Sec.~4.3). All MI values lie in 0.0148 to 0.0170, within estimator noise, so we average over 10 seeds and report that near ties are decided arbitrarily. \textbf{Survey round shift.} Round indicators and covariate shift weights were implemented and tested (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}, E and F); because the 2017 and 2023 covariates are perfectly separable and partly coded differently, pooling still lowers PR-AUC, and code harmonization is a prerequisite for DHS 2019--20. \textbf{Training and test sizes.} The 80/20 hold-out with seed selection was replaced by a fixed seed burden tier 70/30 split and a repeated $5\times5$ cluster grouped cross validation; the burden tier split gives 41 test positives, below the 50 to 55 suggested, because the three largest clusters fell in training and we did not search seeds. \textbf{Events per variable.} EPV is 1.5 to 1.9 for the full feature sets and 4.3 to 5.1 for the compact sets (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}), below the benchmark of 10; the compact sets lose ranking skill, so the best model remains the regularized household plus cluster mean logistic regression with an acknowledged low EPV. We did not reproduce the interview month climate comparison under repeated cross validation.

Earlier review points still hold. GPS displacement (urban up to 2~km, rural up to 5~km) is mitigated by 5~km covariates, not removed. Normalized \texttt{hv005} weights are used in evaluation only, and weighted and unweighted results are reported. Calibration uses only out-of-fold predictions. VIF reduction is restricted to the linear branch. Further limits: 179 positives in total, one survey round per primary model, and results that describe this survey only.

\section{Conclusion}
The pipeline is leakage free and fully auditable, but malaria positivity in MIS~2023 is dominated by a few clusters that household and coarse climate covariates only partly locate. The honest finding is a modest but real lift: under repeated cluster grouped cross validation the best model (regularized logistic regression on household features plus cluster context) reaches PR-AUC $0.080\pm0.032$, about eight times the baseline, with ROC-AUC 0.75 and wide intervals. Survey round pooling, compact feature sets, interview month climate from CHIRPS, ERA5-Land and MODIS, and tuning did not add reliable skill at this sample size. Before Phase~3 the priorities are the full ERA5-Land variable set, harmonized DHS 2019--20 data with a round indicator and covariate shift weights, and the RBC district case series, which would raise the number of events (Table~\ref{tab:data}).

\section{Original Project Proposal}
\label{app:proposal}
This appendix reproduces the original proposal text unchanged, including its original scope (district level monthly early warning from surveillance data). Section~\ref{sec:lim} and Table~\ref{tab:log} explain how and why the scope changed to individual level RDT classification.

\subsection*{Climate Informed Machine Learning for District Level Malaria Early Warning in Rwanda}
\textit{Denys Ntwaritaganzwa, MS EAI, Carnegie Mellon University Africa.}

\subsection*{Abstract}
Malaria remains a leading cause of morbidity in Rwanda despite gains achieved through vector control and treatment programs. District level surveillance recorded through the Rwanda Biomedical Centre Health Management Information System is used mainly to describe past trends rather than anticipate surges, and annual net and spraying allocations ignore short term climate signals. This proposal presents a climate informed machine learning system that forecasts district level malaria risk one to two months ahead across three ordinal levels, normal, elevated, and epidemic, so that officials can position bed nets, spray teams, and drug stocks before, rather than after, a seasonal peak. The system frames prediction as multiclass classification, in which gradient boosted trees and a multinomial logistic regression baseline learn from lagged rainfall, temperature, humidity, vegetation wetness, and case history. Three pilot districts are selected by historical case burden and rainfall driven variability, with additional districts held out to test regional transferability. Validation follows a temporal split that holds out complete malaria seasons and is assessed with balanced accuracy, macro F1 score, and a lead time detection rate, benchmarked against a seasonal persistence baseline. This work directly supports Sustainable Development Goal 3, specifically the target of ending the epidemics of malaria, by translating openly available climate, vegetation, and surveillance data into an operational early warning tool for a resource constrained health system.

\subsection{Introduction and SDG Relevance}
Malaria continues to threaten public health in Rwanda even though control programs have driven major reductions in transmission over the past decade \cite{mohrwanda2019}. A public health bulletin covering 2014 through 2024 shows that incidence and severity among children under five and pregnant women have fluctuated rather than declined steadily \cite{rbc2024}. Surveillance through the District Health Management Information System is compiled after cases have already occurred, so district teams receive information that describes the past rather than a signal that anticipates the near future. Rainfall, temperature, humidity, and vegetation wetness are documented drivers of Anopheles mosquito breeding and Plasmodium falciparum development, and recent studies across Africa show that climate covariates carry predictive information for malaria months ahead of an outbreak \cite{martineau2022,nkiruka2021,khan2024}. This project combines openly available satellite rainfall, temperature, humidity, and vegetation records with historical district level surveillance data to build a machine learning early warning system for Rwanda, turning existing open data into a forecasting tool that district teams can use to plan interventions before, rather than after, a seasonal rise in cases.

\subsection{Selected SDG}
SDG 3, Good Health and Well Being, target 3.3, calls for ending the epidemics of malaria by 2030. The proposed system forecasts district level malaria risk so the national malaria program can allocate long lasting insecticidal nets, indoor residual spraying teams, and antimalarial drug stocks to the districts and months where an elevated or epidemic case load is expected, supporting progress toward elimination rather than only measuring it after the fact.

\subsection{Refined Problem Statement}
The Ministry of Health currently monitors malaria through retrospective descriptive statistics drawn from the District Health Management Information System, aggregated by district and year \cite{rbc2024}. This accurately documents what happened but does not forecast what is likely to happen. Districts with a historically disproportionate case burden are also subject to strong rainfall driven transmission cycles, yet planning cycles for net distribution and spraying are set annually rather than adjusted to short term climate signals, so districts can be under supplied ahead of a severe season or over resourced ahead of a mild one. The deficiency is not a lack of surveillance data but an absence of a predictive layer that converts existing rainfall, temperature, humidity, vegetation, and case history into a forecast with enough lead time, typically one to two months, for procurement and distribution logistics to respond. The initial pilot targets the three districts with the highest documented case burden and the strongest rainfall driven case variability reported in the national malaria bulletin \cite{rbc2024}, and additional districts spanning other agroecological zones will be scored with the same trained models to test regional transferability before any broader rollout is considered. Without such a layer, preventable cases concentrated among children under five and pregnant women continue to occur even where nets, drugs, and spraying capacity already exist within the health system.

\subsection{AI Task Formulation}
The prediction task is a monthly, district level ordinal classification problem. For district $d$ and target month $t$, let $Y(d,t)$ take the value zero for normal incidence, one for elevated incidence, and two for epidemic incidence, defined respectively as below the historical seventy fifth percentile, between the seventy fifth and ninetieth percentile, and above the ninetieth percentile for that calendar month. This extends the above versus below median formulation used in prior work \cite{martineau2022} with an epidemic tier that logistics planners can act on directly. The feature vector $X(d,t)$ combines the most recent one to three months of rainfall, temperature, humidity, and vegetation wetness observations, the district's own case counts from the previous one to three months, and calendar month as a categorical feature to capture residual seasonality. The task is supervised multiclass classification, $f: X \rightarrow Y$, trained separately for lead times of one and two months. A multinomial logistic regression baseline with L2 regularization is compared against a gradient boosted tree ensemble, since gradient boosted trees achieved the highest reported accuracy among classifiers compared in a recent malaria study \cite{khan2024} and natively support multiclass outputs. Hyperparameter tuning uses nested cross validation with a chronological split so no future month predicts an earlier one, and the two most recent complete malaria seasons are held out as a final test set.

\subsection{Initial Data Sources}
\begin{itemize}
\item Rwanda Biomedical Centre DHMIS, via the RBC Public Health Bulletin: monthly and yearly malaria case counts, deaths, and severity by district, 2014 through 2024 \cite{rbc2024}. \url{https://rbc.gov.rw/publichealthbulletin/}
\item WHO Global Health Observatory and World Malaria Report Rwanda country profile, for cross checking national trends \cite{who2024}. \url{https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/malaria-2024-rwa-country-profile}
\item CHIRPS rainfall data, Climate Hazards Center, UC Santa Barbara: daily gridded rainfall at 0.05 degree resolution, 1981 to near present \cite{funk2015}. \url{https://www.chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps}
\item CHIRTS daily temperature dataset from the same center, accessed with the same retrieval tools. \url{https://www.chc.ucsb.edu/data}
\item ERA5 Land hourly reanalysis, Copernicus Climate Data Store: relative humidity and dewpoint temperature, which influence adult mosquito survival beyond rainfall alone \cite{munozsabater2021}. \url{https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/reanalysis-era5-land}
\item MODIS sixteen day vegetation index product, NASA Land Processes Distributed Active Archive Center: a proxy for surface water accumulation and breeding habitat \cite{didan2020}. \url{https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/products/mod13q1v061/}
\item NISR Fifth Population and Housing Census, for district population denominators \cite{nisr2022}. \url{https://statistics.gov.rw/}
\end{itemize}
Full monthly case series for all thirty districts, beyond the aggregated bulletin figures used here, will require a formal request to the Rwanda Biomedical Centre.

\subsection{Expected Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
\item Short historical record and possible reporting changes in the DHMIS over time: eleven years of monthly data limits sample size, so districts will be pooled by agroecological zone before specializing per district, and national totals will be cross checked against WHO figures \cite{who2024}.
\item Coarse climate grid relative to district boundaries and class imbalance across the three risk levels: address with area weighted averaging of pixels per boundary and with balanced class weights, reporting balanced accuracy and macro F1 rather than raw accuracy.
\item Cross border movement, insecticide resistance, and past intervention timing, including spraying and net distribution: none are yet available as structured records, so they are treated as residual error in this pilot, with intervention history to be requested from the Malaria and Other Parasitic Diseases Division for later use as a covariate.
\item Limited district connectivity: run a lightweight, centralized monthly scoring script rather than a real time streaming system.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Preliminary Success Metrics}
Performance is measured with balanced accuracy, macro F1 score, and area under the ROC curve at each lead time, compared against a seasonal persistence baseline that repeats the historical class for each calendar month. An operational lead time detection rate, defined as the proportion of elevated and epidemic months correctly flagged at least one month before onset, will be reported alongside these conventional metrics since it more directly reflects the reduction in stockout risk that the system is intended to support. A preliminary target of seventy five percent balanced accuracy at a one month lead is proposed, to be refined once exploratory analysis of the actual class balance and noise level is complete. Predicted probabilities will also be checked for calibration with a reliability plot, and a short structured feedback session with malaria program staff will assess the usability of the forecast bulletin format.

\subsection{Project Timeline and Deployment Resources}
\begin{itemize}
\item Weeks one to four: problem refinement, literature review, data acquisition across all sources, district boundary mapping, and cleaning of surveillance and climate records.
\item Weeks five to seven: exploratory analysis, lag correlation between covariates and case counts, and development of the multinomial logistic regression baseline.
\item Weeks eight to ten: gradient boosted tree model, nested cross validation, hyperparameter tuning, and held out evaluation against the persistence baseline, with the final days of week ten reserved as a contingency buffer to revisit model choice if performance does not clearly exceed the baseline.
\item Weeks eleven and twelve: prototype monthly bulletin, packaging of the scoring script, documentation, and final presentation.
\end{itemize}
Deployment resources are entirely open source: Python with scikit learn and XGBoost for modeling, retrieval clients for the climate and vegetation archives listed above, geopandas for district boundary handling, a lightweight SQLite database for monthly features, Docker for packaging the scoring script, and a scheduled monthly job to regenerate the bulletin. The extracted dataset is small enough to train on a standard laptop, and a future pilot could run on existing Ministry of Health infrastructure or a modest cloud instance to keep retraining costs low.

\subsection*{Limitations}
This pilot uses a single national surveillance stream and openly available gridded climate and vegetation data, so it cannot yet capture cross border population movement, vector insecticide resistance trends, or the timing of past indoor spraying and net distribution campaigns, all of which independently affect malaria incidence and are not currently available to this project as structured historical records. The eleven year monthly record available per district is short by contemporary machine learning standards, which restricts model complexity and requires cautious interpretation of accuracy figures until more years of data accumulate. Predictions are generated at district resolution and are not intended to guide clinical decisions for individual patients. The coarse resolution of the climate grid relative to district boundaries also introduces averaging error that finer resolution satellite products could reduce in future work.

\subsection*{Ethics Statement}
All data sources proposed for this project are aggregate and publicly released, so no individual patient records are accessed and no additional informed consent process is required beyond what each data provider already applies. Because the output is a probabilistic forecast intended to support, not replace, expert judgment within the national malaria program, the system will present uncertainty alongside each prediction and will be described to end users as decision support rather than an automated allocation mechanism, reducing the risk that a false negative forecast leads to under preparation in an at risk district. Any future extension toward facility level data or historical intervention records would require explicit data sharing agreements with the Rwanda Biomedical Centre and adherence to applicable data protection law.
